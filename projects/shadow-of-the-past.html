Shadow of the Past

A narrative-driven, third-person Unity game where the story is revealed through exploration. I designed and programmed the entire first level by myself.

Overview

You explore Whispering Pines, a forest level, collecting five memory orbs that slowly reveal the story. There’s no linear dialogue. The narrative comes out through the environment, short typed-out cutscenes, and what you discover as you go. Collecting the first memory opens a gate to the second half of the level, and collecting all five triggers the ending.

Level 1 is a complete, playable slice: splash screen, menu, intro cutscene, gameplay, ending cutscene and end screen. I’m continuing to build the next levels.

Player controller

A physics-based third-person controller built on Unity’s Rigidbody, tuned to feel responsive rather than floaty.

  • Camera-relative movement: input is projected onto the camera’s flattened forward and right vectors, so controls always match what the player sees.
  • Double jump with coyote time: players can still jump for a split second after walking off a ledge, which makes platforming feel forgiving.
  • Ground detection from contact normals: only surfaces facing upward count as ground, so walls and steep edges don’t reset jumps. A small downward “stick” force keeps the player stable on slopes.
  • Character facing and animation: the model smoothly turns toward the direction of travel, and animator speed and jump parameters are driven from the Rigidbody’s velocity.

Third-person camera

  • Orbit camera with mouse yaw and pitch, clamped so it can’t flip over or dip under the ground.
  • Scroll-wheel zoom with min and max distance.
  • Camera collision: a sphere cast from the player to the camera pulls the camera in front of trees and walls instead of clipping through them.

Progression & world

  • Memory orbs: idle bob and spin, plus a pulsing glow I made by animating the material’s emission and a point light in code (no post-processing bloom). On pickup they play particles and sound, update the UI and remove themselves cleanly.
  • Game state: a single GameState tracks collected memories and notifies every gate when progress changes.
  • Gates: each gate has its own unlock requirement and slides open with dust and sound. An invisible blocker stops players from jumping past the gate early, and it turns off once the gate is fully open.
  • Level completion: after the fifth memory, the game waits a moment and then fades into the ending cutscene.

Game flow, UI & audio

  • Scene flow: splash → start menu → intro cutscene → level intro → gameplay → ending cutscene → end screen.
  • Persistent screen fader that survives scene loads, fades to black, loads the next scene asynchronously and fades back in. It also resets time scale as a safety net.
  • Typewriter cutscenes and titles using TextMeshPro, with timing that works even when the game is paused.
  • Level intro overlay that disables player and camera control until the title card finishes.
  • HUD: a memory counter (“Memories: 2 / 5”) and timed pop-up messages like “Memory Recovered” and “Path Unlocked.”
  • Audio: jump and landing sounds (landings only play after real falls, not tiny bumps), looping footsteps that fade in and out with movement, and music that persists and fades between scenes.

Design decisions

I kept each system in its own small component: the orb only knows how to be collected, the gate only knows its unlock rule, and the game state connects them. That makes new levels cheap to build, since adding a gate or orb is just placing it in the scene and setting a number in the Inspector. Tuning values like jump force, coyote time, camera limits and fade timing are all exposed in the Inspector, so I could adjust the feel without touching code.

What I learned

Game feel lives in small details: coyote time, filtering out landing sounds on tiny bumps, keeping the camera out of walls. Building every system myself also taught me how to structure a project so it can keep growing, which is exactly what I’m doing with the next levels.
